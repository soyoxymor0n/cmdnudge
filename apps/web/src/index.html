Why this exists

You meant to type an @ for a colleague, and your Mac quit the chat app instead. You took a screenshot last week, and today you work out the keys all over again. And nobody tells you how long it takes until your hands stop slipping.

The hidden cost

Looking it up takes a minute. Finding your place again takes longer.

Each time you stop to search for a shortcut, or ask a chatbot, you leave what you were doing. That's context switching, and the search is the cheap part. The expensive part is getting back into your task. Gloria Mark's studies of office work at UC Irvine found people took about 25 minutes on average to return to an interrupted task (Mark, González and Harris, CHI 2005). A shortcut look-up is a much smaller break, so the numbers below start low. Set them to your day.

32 hours a year

9 minutes a day, over 220 working days. About 4 full working days.

cmdnudge shows the answer in the corner of the screen you're already looking at. There's nothing to search and no task to leave.

Two keyboards

Windows at work, a Mac at home. The same hands.

    The ⌘Q trap

    Same key, different meaning.

    On a German QWERTZ keyboard, @ is AltGr+Q. On a Mac keyboard, the key under your thumb is right ⌘. Your hand does what it always did, and the app is gone.

    Privacy you can read

    It never records what you type.

    An app that sees your keys should prove what it keeps. Every key press goes through one small function first. Letters, numbers, Shift+letter, Space, Return: forgotten on the spot. Only key combinations get through, and even those are only kept as a count of a known habit.

    • Storeda counter per habit and app family: “Copy, in Chat, 12 slips, 30 correct”, and a total per day
    • Never storedwhat you type, which keys, when to the second, window titles, app names
    • Wherea small database on your Mac. You can look at the raw rows, export them or delete them in settings

    Open source shows what the code does. To check that a download matches it, build it yourself from the source.

    Questions

    The short answers.

    Which permissions does it need, and why?

    Input Monitoring, to notice key combinations at all. Accessibility, so the guard can stop a right ⌘Q and translate mode can press the Mac shortcut for you. macOS asks once; you can take both back any time in System Settings.

    Which keyboard layouts work?

    {{mapped}}, picked from your Mac's input source: US, German QWERTZ (Germany and Austria), Swiss German and Swiss French, British, French AZERTY, Belgian, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Swedish and Finnish, Norwegian, Danish, Polish, Czech and Hungarian. For each one cmdnudge knows where your hands go for @, €, braces and the rest, measured from the Windows and Mac layouts themselves. On any other layout shortcuts like Ctrl+C still work, because the app reads the keycaps from the layout; only the AltGr characters are missing, and the app has a link to ask for yours.

    I use a PC keyboard on my Mac. Does that matter?

    Yes, and cmdnudge knows it. On an Apple keyboard your Alt key is ⌘; on a PC keyboard the Windows key is ⌘ and Alt is ⌥. The hints follow the keyboard you pick in settings.

    What about remote desktop, virtual machines and the terminal?

    In remote desktop apps and VMs, Windows shortcuts are the right ones, so cmdnudge stays quiet there. In terminals Ctrl+C means something of its own, so shortcut hints are off and only the @-style typing hints remain. You can mute any app family.

    Does it change what my keys do?

    Not by default. It only shows hints. Translate mode presses the Mac shortcut for you as well; strict mode holds the old shortcut back until you use the Mac one. The guard is the one thing on by default: it stops right ⌘Q when it came from the @ habit.

    Is it free? Where do I get it?

    Free and open source under the MIT licence. Right now you build it from the source on GitHub, which takes a few minutes with the steps in the readme.

    Does anything leave my Mac?

    No. There is no account and no server in this version. The counts live in a small database on your Mac, and you can export or delete them in settings. If you use two Macs, you can point both at a folder you already sync, like iCloud Drive, and each adds the other's counts.
